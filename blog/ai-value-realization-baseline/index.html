When a Great AI KPI Tells an Incomplete Story

Kate Warman · Value Realization Pillar Champion, Human-Centric AI Council

There is a lot of pressure right now to demonstrate value from AI. Understandably, we tend to gravitate toward the things we can count: time saved, cost reduced, adoption rates, productivity gains.

Those things matter, but they don't always tell the whole story.

I've been thinking about this through the lens of an AI project I led a couple of years ago. We built a job-posting generator to solve a problem we'd been wrestling with for years: creating and maintaining quality, consistent job postings at a global scale.

We had tried creating a global library of job postings that recruiters could reuse. In theory, it made perfect sense. In practice, roles and messaging changed faster than we could maintain it. Old postings got copied and reused, sometimes carrying forward problems that were already there.

We knew what we wanted to fix. We were dealing with:

  • Too much time spent creating postings: Recruiters were starting from old postings or a blank page and spending valuable time creating and refining content.
  • Outdated and inconsistent content: Roles and messaging changed faster than we could maintain the library, and postings varied across geographies, job levels and recruiters.
  • A disconnect from job architecture: What we advertised externally didn't always align with the role the organization had actually designed.
  • Missed brand and EVP opportunities: The messaging Marketing had carefully developed wasn't consistently making its way into what candidates saw.
  • A weak starting point for recruiters: Recruiters needed a stronger foundation for job-launch conversations with hiring managers.

AI gave us a different way to solve the problem. Instead of trying to maintain a static library, we could generate a current posting when we needed one, in nine languages, using a consistent framework.

And it worked. We estimated that the solution reduced the time required to create a posting by a whopping 80%.

80%! That's a fantastic outcome. It's exactly the kind of clear, compelling KPI we want when we're trying to demonstrate the value of AI.

And it wasn't the only outcome. The solution also:

  • Created global consistency and stronger alignment with job architecture by anchoring postings to the role the organization had actually designed, so the same role was represented consistently across countries and regions.
  • Reduced risk by replacing outdated, inconsistent content with a more standardized approach.
  • Strengthened alignment with Marketing by consistently bringing our brand, tone of voice and EVP into candidate-facing content.
  • Empowered recruiters by giving them a strong starting point for job-launch conversations. If a hiring manager wanted to substantially change the posting, the recruiter had a basis for asking whether we were still hiring the role that had actually been defined.

That's the point. The 80% wasn't the wrong KPI. It was a great KPI.

It just wasn't the whole value story.

Your baseline shapes the value you can demonstrate

We intentionally designed the solution to deliver all of those outcomes. The problem was that we hadn't established equally strong baselines for all of them.

Time was relatively easy. We could ask recruiters how long creating a posting used to take and compare that with the new experience. We knew the other problems existed too, but much of our evidence was anecdotal. That made it much harder to demonstrate how much we'd improved consistency, brand alignment, reduced risk or empowered recruiters.

An 80% improvement also makes for a much cleaner KPI than “recruiters were better equipped to challenge role misalignment.”

But the fact that something is harder to measure doesn't make it less valuable.

That's the lesson I would take into an AI project today. A baseline isn't just the number you're hoping to move. It's understanding the current state well enough that you know what you want to change and what evidence you'll need later to demonstrate whether you changed it.

Three questions I would ask now

If I were starting that project again, I wouldn't change what we built. I would spend more time upfront answering three questions:

1. What problem are we actually trying to solve? Understand the process, the data and the experience of the people doing the work. Then decide whether AI is the solution, part of the solution, or whether something else needs to change too.

2. So what? If we solve it, what gets better? Is it faster? Cheaper? Higher quality? A better experience? Less risk? And, importantly, why does that matter to the business or the people it serves?

3. How will we know it worked? What do we know about the current state, and what evidence will tell us later that it improved? Some of that evidence will be quantitative. Some won't be. Candidate and employee surveys, stakeholder feedback, open-text comments, interviews and focus groups can reveal things that aren't visible in operational data alone.

The numbers may tell you where to look. The narrative may tell you what to fix.

This isn't about turning every benefit into a dollar amount or spending months creating the perfect measurement framework before anyone is allowed to experiment with AI.

It's about thinking upfront about the value we expect to create, so we're better equipped to recognize and demonstrate it afterward.

AI value realization doesn't start when the technology goes live. It starts when we understand where we are, where we want to go, and why getting there matters.

Because you can hit your AI target, move your KPI and still not capture the full value of the solution.
